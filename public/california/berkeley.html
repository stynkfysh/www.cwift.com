Homes in Berkeley

Berkeley is known for early-1900s brown-shingle and Craftsman houses, with much of North Berkeley rebuilt after the 1923 fire, and for designs by architects such as Bernard Maybeck and Julia Morgan. The flats of West and South Berkeley hold small bungalows, Victorians and cottages on narrow lots, while the Elmwood and Claremont add larger shingled and revival-style homes. The Berkeley Hills, Thousand Oaks and Cragmont climb onto steep, wooded lots with bay views and stairs from the street. Condos and small apartment buildings cluster near downtown, Southside and University Avenue.

What moves home values in Berkeley

Hill homes sit in Berkeley's fire zone, where roof type, vegetation clearance, narrow streets and insurance cost affect demand; exterior photos and lot views shape the comparison.

The Hayward Fault runs along the base of the Berkeley Hills; houses in its mapped zone carry disclosures, and foundation bolting or retrofit work in photos and permits is noted.

Condos near downtown and Southside depend on HOA dues, reserves and building age; association records are reviewed, and units are matched with sales in similar Berkeley buildings.

Original shingle-style details, from redwood paneling to leaded windows, matter to Berkeley buyers; photos of intact interiors versus remodeled ones help match the right comparable sales.

Each of these is documented from your photos, permits and records and reflected in the comparable-sales adjustments.

Questions from Berkeley

Will the backyard cottage behind my late mom's Berkeley house count in the appraisal?

Yes, but how it counts depends on permits. A permitted ADU adds value and is compared with Berkeley sales that also have a second unit. An unpermitted cottage is described and given the contribution buyers actually pay for, which is usually less. Send photos of the cottage inside and out, plus any permit or rental paperwork, so it can be classified correctly.

Does an appraiser need to come to the house in Berkeley?

No. The appraiser works from your photos, county records, listing history and recent sales nearby, and the report states that no site visit was made. If your attorney or a court needs an inspected appraisal, ask before ordering.
